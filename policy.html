Push one · alone

Sole author · November 29, 2024

Memorandum to Governor Brian Kemp

Before any cohort, before anyone taught me the format: a memorandum opposing legislation to mandate DNA collection from misdemeanor offenders, argued on four fronts — Fourth Amendment doctrine under Maryland v. King, the equity consequences for minority and low-income populations, the fiscal load of processing and storage, and the thin evidence that it reduces crime at all. This is the writing sample that got me into the Urban Leadership Fellowship. Written alone.

The constitutional argumentMaryland v. King upheld DNA collection for serious offenses on the gravity of the crime. Extend it to minor, non-violent misdemeanors and that rationale collapses — a search without sufficient probable cause.
The recommendationReconsider the legislation in favor of more targeted and equitable approaches to public safety.

Push two · Georgia

Summer cohort · Atlanta, Georgia · 2025

A Policy Blueprint for Healthy School Meals in Georgia

The final policy of the Urban Leadership Fellowship reform cohort, written with Senge Ngalame, Josh Thomas and Brandon Isome. The deck is addressed to Rep. Eric Bell II of the Georgia House, and it puts Clayton County under the microscope. The title slide carries all four of our names. The presentation itself is below — it is the source for both.

My section was the science. Where the memo had to explain why additives harm children — the mechanism the entire policy rests on — that chapter is mine: “Scientific Mechanisms of Harm: How Additives Affect the Body.” It reads the neurobiology past the label, through the Southampton Trial in The Lancet, Stevens et al. on dye loads in children’s beverages, and Title 21 CFR §101.22, to the plain conclusion that a food label does not reveal the biochemical interplay — but a pediatric clinic in a low-income zip code does.

We ignore the silent molecular violence inside our school lunchrooms if we fail to recognize these mechanisms. — from my section of the final memorandum

The two questions the policy set out to answer

  1. What are the health impacts of food additives on children and low-income Black and Brown communities?
  2. How effective are current state-level regulations, public education campaigns, and monitoring systems at mitigating the public health risks associated with food additives?

What the blueprint actually does

Costs a real pilotA twenty-school Georgia pilot at $12.6M, broken out: $4.6M vendor transition grants, $2M market development, $2.5M staff training and kitchen readiness, $600K evaluation and impact tracking, $3M inflation buffer.
Names the countiesClayton against Thomas — median household income, poverty against Georgia’s 13.6%, meal cost, and the dollars each county needs to close its own food gap. One in five Georgia children face hunger.
Tracks the lawActive state food-additive bills by chemical, against the Environmental Working Group’s 2025 tracker, with the comparative state models beside them.
Screens the vendorsFour clean-label suppliers judged on a cost-neutral $3.75 per meal, with a qualification bar that eliminates Red #3, Yellow #6 and BHA/BHT, and requires traceability, allergen data and staff training.
Prices the alternativeThe healthcare cost of food insecurity — 70% of affected households report a diet-related condition, at $1,834 more per food-insecure adult — and the measured effect of healthy versus standard vendors on test scores.
Hands over next stepsAn explicit list for the Georgia Department of Education and school boards: identify the twenty pilot schools, issue the RFP for clean-label vendors, run community outreach across a 12–18 month window, and track results.

Sourced to ACS 2023, USDA Economic Research Service, the Environmental Working Group’s 2025 food bill tracker, the Georgia Hunger Study 2023, and the National Bureau of Economic Research.

Push three · Connecticut

Connecticut Siting Council · Docket No. 516 and the 516R remand · 2023–2026

The public record, made readable

United Illuminating moved to relocate and rebuild its 115-kV transmission lines along the Metro-North corridor through Fairfield and Bridgeport. I built the archive: every filing collected, indexed and explained in plain English — the application, the transcripts, the final decision, and the 516R remand — so a resident could follow what was happening to their own street without a lawyer.

The whole docket325 filings archived and explained, each one source-linked, one tap from the plain-English version to the original paper.
Why it mattersThe case shows exactly how the system moves when a citizen pushes back with paper — which is the only kind of pressure a siting council is obliged to answer.
How it endedOn October 16, 2025 the Siting Council voted 5–3 to deny the application. United Illuminating asked it to reconsider; on February 5, 2026 the Council rejected that appeal and closed Docket 516R. Reporting on the decision recorded the community’s unified front as a major factor in it.

Outcome sourced to CT Mirror, October 16, 2025 and CT Mirror, February 5, 2026.

Why both, and why the same year

Food policy and utility siting look like different rooms until you notice who lives beside the substation and who eats the school lunch. Both pushes ask the same question — who absorbs the cost of a decision they were not in the room for — and both answer it the same way: get the numbers, name the sources, publish the record, and hand somebody with authority a list they can actually act on. The DNA memo was constitutional and went to a governor. Georgia was legislative and went through a fellowship cohort to a state representative. Connecticut was regulatory and went through a public archive to a siting council. The Connecticut General Assembly cited the work that October, and the State of Georgia and the City of Bridgeport followed in December.
