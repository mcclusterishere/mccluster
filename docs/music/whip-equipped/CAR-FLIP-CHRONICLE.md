# Car-flip chronicle — first road-years recollections

Source intake: **owner recollection received 2026-11-03**.

Matthew McCluster remembers these three transactions as part of the period when he was buying and selling campers and vans, working on the road, and keeping a rental business going. He places them around **2024**, with uncertainty. They are an initial source chronicle, separate from the fictional events in the [Whip Equipped screenplay](./SCREENPLAY.md) and its [authoritative story guide](../WHIP-EQUIPPED-STORY.md).

The accompanying [Road Years](./ROAD-YEARS.md) dramatization may develop scenes from these recollections. Newly written dialogue, transitions, and dramatic action must remain identifiable as adaptation rather than become evidence of what happened.

No photographs, receipts, travel records, or transaction documents accompanied **this batch of three recollections**. This does not describe the availability of sources elsewhere in the project. Prices, locations, sequence, and impressions below retain the qualifications in the owner's account.

## Working sequence

The owner thinks the inexpensive towable camper came first. The G20 followed, traveling from Michigan through Georgia and Connecticut before its sale in Long Island. A prospective buyer first encountered through the G20 became the customer for a later green Econoline around Boston. That lead connects the second and third stories even though they concern different vehicles and sales.

The stable IDs below organize the entries. Their **2024** component is an approximate recalled period, not verification of a transaction year or an exact chronological numbering system.

## FLIP-2024-01-camper — the $100 camper

The owner recalls buying a towable camper for approximately **$100** and selling it in Georgia for about **$2,500**. Majid and Majid's younger brother helped with the transport. The younger brother received approximately **$600** to tow it to Georgia. The account does not specify how the two helpers divided the work or establish the camper's starting location.

Fuel was a substantial part of the transaction. The owner first recalled **at least $300**, then mentioned **$400**. Those figures support alternative calculations; they do not establish that $400 was the maximum spent. The low purchase price therefore should not be presented as the entire cost of acquiring and delivering the camper.

He thinks this happened before the G20, during the same approximately 2024 period. Its make, model, year, size, condition, buyer, and holding period are still unrecorded. The evidence supports a camper that was towed; it does not establish a particular motorhome or trailer model.

An adaptation can develop the effort and relationships involved in moving it, but the precise route, towing complications, conversations, and roadside incidents would be newly written unless later recollections or records supply them.

## FLIP-2024-02-g20 — living inside the inventory

The G20 cost approximately **$800 out the door** in Michigan. The owner recalls spending about **$100** on the flight there, describing his initial purchase-and-flight outlay as roughly **$900**. He also mentions AAA towing, without specifying its route, reason, coverage, or cost. The current record does not treat that service as free or assign it a guessed charge.

The van had a sleeping arrangement in the back. It served simultaneously as a vehicle for sale, transportation, and somewhere to live. The owner remembers spending approximately **four days** in it during the road period, sleeping inside and doing DoorDash work along the way to obtain gas money. Cash was low. Majid stayed on the phone to help him remain awake and provide reassurance about the flip.

The working route is **Michigan → Georgia → Connecticut → Long Island**, followed by a train back to Connecticut after the sale. The owner recalls selling the G20 for about **$2,500**, in summertime, and thinks the year was 2024. The four-day recollection does not establish the entire ownership period or a precisely measured driving leg.

During the retelling, he briefly said Rhode Island and then returned to Long Island. **Long Island is the working sale location.** The earlier wording remains an unresolved verbal slip; it does not justify adding another trip or another sale.

The manufacturer's identity beyond the owner's G20 label, the model year, exact dates, fuel, train fare, repairs, fees, towing costs, and DoorDash amounts remain open. The train return belongs to this story and should not be transferred to the later Boston transaction. Exact phone dialogue or a particular roadside emergency in a dramatization would be invented unless separately sourced.

## FLIP-2024-03-econoline — the Boston buyer

The next vehicle was a **green Ford Econoline van**, described using Ford 150 / Econoline 150 wording. **E-150 is a tentative working designation**, not a verified registration entry. Its year is unresolved: the owner considered approximately 1993, 1998, and 2003 while making clear that he did not remember. None should be selected as fact.

He recalls paying **$1,500** and selling it around Boston for approximately **$2,600 or $2,700**, about a month or possibly less after purchase. The buyer had originally been a lead for the G20. After that vehicle sold, the owner looked for similar vans and brought him the Econoline instead.

### The encounter, as remembered

The buyer is anonymized as **the Boston buyer**. In extensive phone conversations, the owner says the man assumed he was white because of his speech and regional manner. The buyer made racist remarks, including an account of racially directed violence. That alleged violence was something the buyer reportedly described; the owner does not present himself as its witness.

The owner recalls playing along with racist joking before they met. In person, the buyer was surprised and questioned whether this was the same person from the phone. The owner asked whether his identity would interfere with completing their business. The buyer became apologetic and offered to purchase cannabis as an apology. The owner recognized the stereotype in the offer and accepted it.

At a Massachusetts dispensary, the buyer purchased approximately **$100 of cannabis for him**. This was a buyer-funded personal gift, separate from the van's stated cash sale price. It was not another $100 of cash proceeds or a cost paid by the owner.

The owner then accompanied the buyer to his home while helping with vehicle transport. Who drove which vehicle, the exact route, and the home's location are not established. Concerned about his safety, he contacted his younger sister, shared his location, and asked her to check that he got home safely.

At the home, the buyer invited him into a shed to demonstrate making cigarettes and cannabis cigarettes. The owner remembers apprehension but went to look. The encounter turned out to be the offered demonstration, and he felt reassured. His concern does not establish that the buyer planned an attack, that a confrontation occurred, or that anyone carried a weapon.

He remembers the man as very apologetic and feels he **may have changed the buyer's heart**. That is his retrospective interpretation of the encounter, not evidence of a lasting change in the buyer's beliefs. A dramatization should preserve that distinction, the owner's mixed reactions, and the anonymization. It should not add invented threats or turn the encounter into proof that racism was cured.
