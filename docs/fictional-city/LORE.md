# McCluster City — lore

**Revision: November 3, 2026.** This is the readable companion to the canonical
[district manifest](../../core/data/fictional-city/district.v1.json): eight shops,
24 newly written adult residents, and three existing Whip Equipped characters at
the garage. Each person has a biography and three playable conversation topics.
The current set contains 81 topics and 486 spoken lines, plus greetings and farewells.

**Schema revision 2 (same date) widens the city.** Four new neighborhoods around the original
block and the Equity Uprise island add 21 businesses and civic buildings (Wiggers Anonymous,
Thugged Out Apparel, a church, the School of Hard Knocks, the real Harding High School, a
private hospital, the real Bridgeport Police Department headquarters, a bail-bond office whose back-counter
fixers sell a laminated menu of payoffs, and fourteen more joke businesses), 50 new residents, ten
households and 24 more goods: 104 more topics and 624 more spoken lines. See
[The wider city](#the-wider-city-new-neighborhoods-people-and-businesses) below.

The city’s working name is **McCluster City**. Its commercial blocks take inspiration
from Bridgeport without replacing the real place or pretending to reproduce its streets.
The owner’s premise is a neighborhood filled with their joke businesses, where an ad
leads to a shop, the shop leads to people, and the people have reasons to know one another.
The garage is the familiar place where those stories meet Whip Equipped.

### The campaign commercial

Mike wants a heroic walk and a speech with impressive endurance. Cleo wants to film him
listening to one real request. Ada’s notebook supplies the request: campaign signs are
blocking Mara’s loading space. Noah sees a measurable promise. Hugo has already told
both the tea shop and the campaign that they have an exclusive advertising arrangement.
The scene can move from wordplay into a small test of character: can Mike complete the
favor before turning it into another announcement? Lin’s matter-of-fact reaction supplies
a better ending than applause would.

### One van, three descriptions

Nadia booked a vehicle for a night-shift promo. Kwame booked a promo with a night vehicle.
Amina knows the actual vehicle still has an ordinary working schedule, and Tomas needs
time to prepare it. Beatrice holds the keys. The apparent conflict survives because
everybody repeats a different description of the same object. Gunny’s garage advice is
to ask who actually agreed. The comic resolution begins with plain questions about time,
destination, and what goes inside; it does not require anyone to become a fool.

### The empty sneaker plinth

June refuses to fill the original-pair display with something she cannot substantiate.
Leo would rather show a good repair. Samira thinks an honest gap is more interesting than
a confident invention. Hugo sees unused advertising space. A visitor can hear competing
versions of what makes a shop valuable without creating a new wearer in the established
Robbery 1s story. Replica purchases remain replica purchases. The recovered cast and
original curse are not rewritten by a checkout.

### The guarantee finally gets tested

Lin can stop a passerby with the slogan. Eli can find out what that person actually likes.
Mara has to do the less glamorous work of honoring the guarantee when a cup disappoints.
Hugo wants the response filmed; Mara wants to solve it before it becomes a campaign.
The situation lets the outrageous ad and a genuinely hospitable business exist together.
Lin’s desire to direct the next commercial gives the story somewhere to go after the joke.

### Who did the shoot?

The camera crew, director, lighting technician, sound worker, wardrobe specialist, and
producer can all answer the title truthfully. The client thanks the camera brand. Deposits,
free-work requests, and an expanding brief generate the conflict, following the recovered
production-industry premise. Owen’s biscuit tin and Vik’s missing adapter turn invisible
work into memorable details. The new conversations are not represented as recovered lyrics.

### The interview that keeps getting interrupted

Tasha wants to tell her own work stories. Darnell wants to listen but has built a persona
out of interruption and urgency. Priya can protect the context, while Hugo needs to offer
an actual hosting fee rather than the word exposure. This is a way to give a broad joke
business a person whose goals matter beyond the sign outside it.

#### Mara Costa

**Owner and reluctant face of the guarantee** · 39 · Recife, Brazil

Mara grew up helping a relative run a neighborhood snack counter in Recife, where remembering an order mattered more than having a slogan. Years later she opened the city shop with a small menu and borrowed recording equipment. One furious practice take became the commercial everybody wanted. She is proud of the attention and embarrassed that people sometimes leave quoting the ad without remembering the tea. Mara keeps a notebook of regulars’ preferences behind the till and quietly replaces drinks customers dislike. Her ambition is to make the guarantee honest without becoming a bland version of herself. She trusts Eli with the stock and Lin with the performance; she needs both of them to tell her when she has confused being memorable with being right.

**Temperament:** Blunt, hospitable, ambitious, and capable of apologizing without losing her nerve.

**Greeting:** “Welcome. The tea is hot. The guarantee is unnecessarily personal.”

**Conversation — Explain that guarantee.**

> **You:** So what happens if I don’t like the tea?

> **Mara Costa:** I make you another one. I run a shop, not a hostage situation.

> **You:** Your sign has a different energy.

> **Mara Costa:** We guarantee you'll like it, or else fuck you.

> **You:** There it is.

> **Mara Costa:** And there’s the kettle. One of those things actually solves your problem.

**Conversation — How did the VHS ad happen?**

> **You:** Did you deliberately make the ad look thirty years old?

> **Mara Costa:** At first? No. That was the camera we had.

> **You:** And the dramatic zoom?

> **Mara Costa:** The table moved. Cleo called it a creative decision, then sent an invoice.

> **You:** Did the ad work?

> **Mara Costa:** People know every word. Now I’m trying the radical next step of selling them tea.

**Conversation — What do you want from this block?**

> **You:** PressureTV says you’re taking over the neighborhood.

> **You:** You don’t want an empire?

> **Mara Costa:** I want Eli to get a lunch break and Lin to stop shouting at delivery drivers.

> **You:** That sounds smaller.

> **Mara Costa:** It sounds achievable. Buy a cup and we can fund the revolution slowly.

**Farewell:** “Come back. Tell me what you actually liked this time.”

### Neighborhoods

**Precinct Square** (`civic-center`). The city’s civic heart, in the sense that something is technically beating. St. Copay’s, a private hospital, measures its emergency-room wait in fiscal quarters. The church shares a parking lot with the bank, a sermon schedule with the bank’s overdraft calendar, and a boot technician with Bail Mary’s expediting desk. The School of Hard Knocks sits behind an ivy-covered wall it imported from a richer city, and directly across the square, in a former dentist’s office, Wiggers Anonymous meets every night with fresh coffee and very low expectations.

**Cul-de-Sadness** (`northside-residential`). Ten households on curving streets that were supposed to become a golf community until the golf course became a retention pond. Three enormous houses at the end of the loop belong to families who send their sons to the School of Hard Knocks; seven ordinary houses belong to people who work at the hospital, the church and the shops, and who are tired of having their recycling bins tagged with the words BOND ST WILLY WHACKERS in very neat handwriting. **Rustbucket Wharf** (`workshop-waterfront`). The industrial harbor on the far side of the city’s working waterfront: cranes nobody operates, a laundromat with an unusually clean cash flow, a car wash that returns most of its cars, a self-storage complex where several units have mailboxes, a pawn shop that buys gold teeth, a motel that rents by the hour and judges by the minute, and the regional headquarters of an essential-oil pyramid with three employees and nine thousand executives. Whip Equipped knows every driveway here and pretends not to.

### 9. Wiggers Anonymous

**Twelve-step recovery group** — Hi, my name is Todd, and it has been 2,211 days since I called anybody “fam.”

Parcel `civic-center/wiggers-anonymous`.

A recovery fellowship for suburban white guys who built their whole personality out of a cartoon of Black and hip-hop culture they learned from a music video and a gas-station energy drink. Folding chairs, burnt coffee, a donated whiteboard, and a sign-in sheet where half the members still write their rap names.

Gordon Pfeiffer founded the group after the night he tried to settle a homeowners’-association dispute with a freestyle, lost, and was asked by his own daughter to “please just be a dentist.” He was once one. So were Todd and Derek. The three of them rent the old dental office on Precinct Square, still with the reclining chair, and run meetings seven nights a week. The rules are simple: you introduce yourself by your legal name, you surrender one accessory per milestone, and nobody is allowed to describe a personal problem as “the streets” if the problem is a parking ticket in a cul-de-sac. The program does not target anybody’s culture. It targets four grown men who keep mistaking somebody else’s culture for a costume, and one man the School of Hard Knocks pays to stay that way.

**Interior:** A circle of folding chairs, a coffee urn, a whiteboard of anniversary dates, and a donation bin full of surrendered chains, flat-brim hats and one keytar.

**Count note:** The owner asked for three suffering members and then named four jobs (drug dealer, Hitman, Equity Uprise, Thugged Out Apparel). The fellowship has four, one per job; Gordon calls the fourth chair “the overflow.”

**The Twelve Steps**

1. We admitted we were powerless over the bass, and that our lives had become a mixtape nobody asked for.
2. Came to believe that a Power greater than ourselves, usually our mothers’ disappointment, could restore us to khakis.
3. Made a decision to turn our wardrobe over to the care of someone who has met us.
4. Made a searching and fearless inventory of our hats.
5. Admitted to the group and to one other human being the exact nature of our street name.
6. Were entirely ready to have our legal names restored.
7. Humbly asked our barber to remove our shortcomings, and the lightning bolt.
8. Made a list of all persons we had called “bro” in a voice that was not ours, and became willing to apologize to them all.
9. Made direct amends wherever possible, except when doing so would require another freestyle.
10. Continued to take personal inventory, and when we said “deadass,” promptly admitted it.
11. Sought through quiet reflection to improve our conscious contact with our actual hometown.
12. Having had a spiritual awakening as the result of these steps, we tried to carry this message to other guys in cargo shorts and a durag, and to practice these principles in all our group chats.

**Ad cards**
