To: Governor Brian Kemp

From: Matthew McCluster

Subject: Opposition to Legislation Mandating DNA Collection from Misdemeanor Offenders

Date: Friday, November 29, 2024

Executive Summary

The proposed legislation to mandate DNA collection from individuals convicted of misdemeanor offenses presents significant constitutional, ethical, and practical challenges. While DNA databases have been instrumental in solving serious crimes, extending this practice to misdemeanors infringes upon civil liberties, may violate Fourth Amendment protections, and risks exacerbating systemic inequities. Moreover, the expansion would impose substantial logistical and financial burdens without demonstrable enhancements to public safety.

Constitutional and Privacy Concerns

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures. In Maryland v. King (2013), the U.S. Supreme Court upheld DNA collection for serious offenses, likening it to fingerprinting during booking procedures. However, this rationale was based on the gravity of the crimes involved. Applying DNA collection to misdemeanors—often minor, non-violent offenses—lacks similar justification and may constitute an overreach of governmental authority, potentially violating constitutional rights through invasive searches without sufficient probable cause.

Impact on Marginalized Communities

Empirical evidence indicates that expanding DNA collection to misdemeanor offenses disproportionately affects minority and low-income populations, exacerbating existing disparities within the criminal justice system. This practice risks perpetuating systemic biases, leading to over-surveillance and potential misuse of genetic information against vulnerable groups. Such outcomes are antithetical to the principles of equity and social justice.

Logistical and Financial Implications

Incorporating misdemeanor offenders into DNA databases would significantly increase the volume of samples processed, straining resources and potentially causing backlogs. The financial costs associated with the collection, analysis, and storage of additional DNA samples are considerable and may divert funds from other critical public safety initiatives. Allocating resources to expand DNA databases in this manner may not yield proportional benefits in crime reduction.

Efficacy in Crime Reduction

While DNA databases have been effective in solving serious crimes, there is limited evidence to suggest that including misdemeanor offenders’ DNA significantly enhances law enforcement capabilities. The marginal benefits do not justify the extensive costs and potential civil liberties infringements. Focusing on more targeted approaches may prove more effective in promoting public safety without compromising individual rights.

Conclusion

Balancing public safety with the protection of individual rights is paramount. The proposed legislation to mandate DNA collection from misdemeanor offenders poses substantial constitutional challenges, risks exacerbating social inequities, and imposes significant logistical and financial burdens without clear evidence of its effectiveness in crime reduction. It is recommended that this legislation be reconsidered in favor of more targeted and equitable approaches to enhancing public safety.

References

  • Giannaros, C. (2015). Why Collection of Arrestee DNA Violates the Fourth Amendment. JURIST. jurist.org
  • National Institute of Justice. (2009). Debating DNA Collection. nij.ojp.gov
  • Maryland v. King, 569 U.S. 435 (2013).
